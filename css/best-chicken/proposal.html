Section 1: Project Title

Best Chicken in Chicago

The title says what the site is about in plain language. A visitor can tell it is a guide to Chicago fried chicken, not a menu app, a delivery site, or a personal resume. The three restaurants, Harold's Chicken, Uncle Remus, and Shark's Fish & Chicken, are named on the home page so the topic is specific.

Section 2: Project Type

Interest Website

I am choosing an Interest Website. The topic is Chicago chicken, focused on three well-known spots: Harold's, Uncle Remus, and Shark's. It is not a personal website, a single-event site, or a collection catalog. It also is not an online store. The site explains the food and the history. It does not take orders.

Section 3: Project Background

Best Chicken in Chicago will be a static informational website about three chicken restaurants people in the city actually argue over. Harold's Chicken started in 1950, when Harold Pierce, who had come to Chicago from Alabama, opened a shack on the South Side. The exact corner is remembered differently, so the site will say that instead of inventing one address. Uncle Remus was opened in 1969 by Gus Rickette and Mary Rickette, who had come from Mississippi. Their first Uncle Remus was at 210 S. Cicero Avenue, after earlier chicken shacks on the West Side. Shark's Fish & Chicken is the newer of the three. It grew up in Chicago around the late 1990s and early 2000s and is known for fish and lemon-pepper wings as much as for chicken. I chose this topic because these are the names people say when they talk about the best chicken in Chicago, and each one has a real story.

The site will explain what each place is known for, how a first order works, and how mild sauce connects Harold's and Uncle Remus while Shark's leans on seasoning and fish. Pages will share one header, one navigation bar, and one footer. Styling will come from an external CSS file, including a layout that works on a phone and on a laptop. JavaScript will stay beginner level: a mobile menu button, and a short "help me choose" set of questions on the Compare page that suggests one of the three restaurants. Nothing will be stored on a server. There will be no online ordering, accounts, payments, or store-locator database.

Section 4: Target Audience

Audience 1: Chicago residents who already have a favorite

Who they are. People who live here and already say Harold's, Uncle Remus, or Shark's is the best. That includes students who grew up on one side of the city and have not tried the other two.

What they will look for. The history of their spot, what the other two do differently, and a fair comparison they can send to a friend during the argument.

Why the site is useful for them. Each restaurant has its own page, and the Compare page puts sauce, sides, and a first order next to each other. They can read it without downloading an app or placing an order.

Audience 2: Visitors and people new to Chicago

Who they are. Out-of-town guests, new residents, and students who just moved here and do not know how a Chicago chicken order works.

What they will look for. Which place to try first, what mild sauce is, and how to order. At Harold's, a common first plate is a mixed half chicken with fries, white bread, and mild sauce. At Uncle Remus, wings with fries and mild sauce are the signature. At Shark's, lemon-pepper wings or a fish dinner is the usual starting point.

Why the site is useful for them. The home page and Compare page explain the difference before they stand at the counter. The site does not pretend to sell the food or list every location in a live map.

Section 5: Planned Website Pages

The site will have six pages. Every page will use the same navigation: Home, Harold's, Uncle Remus, Shark's, Compare, and Contact.

1. Home

A short welcome that names the three restaurants and says this is a guide, not an order form. Three cards link to Harold's (1950, South Side), Uncle Remus (1969, West Side), and Shark's (lemon pepper and fish). A button leads to the Compare page.

2. Harold's Chicken

The story of Harold Pierce and Hilda Pierce, the 1950 shack, and why many locations still feel different from each other. The page will describe chicken served over fries and white bread, with mild or hot sauce, and the phrase "fried hard." It will also note that some details, including the exact first corner, are disputed, so those lines will be labeled as such.

3. Uncle Remus

The story of Gus Rickette and Mary Rickette, from Mississippi to West Side chicken shacks, and the 1969 opening of Uncle Remus. The page will explain that the name came from a leftover restaurant sign, not from a plan to use the old folk-tale character. It will focus on the mild sauce, wings, and the Austin location on Madison Street that is still part of the business. The business is still in the family.

4. Shark's Fish & Chicken

How Shark's fits the same Chicago chicken conversation even though it is newer. The page will cover fish, chicken wings, and lemon pepper, and it will say the chain took shape in the Chicago area by the early 2000s. I will not invent a founder story if the sources do not agree. The point of the page is what a first visit looks like and how Shark's differs from the two older shacks.

5. Compare

A simple table, built in HTML, with three columns: Harold's, Uncle Remus, and Shark's. Rows will cover when it started, the part of the city it is tied to, the signature order, and the sauce or seasoning. Under the table, a short JavaScript feature will ask two or three questions, such as whether the person wants mild sauce or lemon pepper, and then suggest one restaurant. The result stays on the page. It is not saved anywhere.

6. Contact

A note that "best" is an argument, plus a form for a favorite order or a correction. Fields: name, email, and message. JavaScript will require all three and check that the email contains an @ symbol. If the form is valid, the browser will open an email. Messages will not be saved in a database.
